--- title: "Six Weeks at Physio" subtitle: "A memoir from the 2026 MBL Cell Physiology course" description: "Six weeks of cell biology, late-night experiments, scientific immersion, and reflections on becoming a biologist." date: 2026-08-04 ---

From June 15 to July 26, I attended the Cell Physiology (Physio) summer school at the Marine Biological Laboratory (MBL) with 26 other PhD students and postdocs from all over the world. While the course had nothing to do with marine biology, we surely benefited from the ocean views and the diverse biological environment. We explored modern cell biology in depth using microscopic, biochemical, and computational approaches. The course was structured as 2 “skills weeks” followed by two 2-week rotations. In each rotation, three PIs would bring intentionally immature research projects from their labs, along with their graduate students/postdocs as TAs, to explore with us. I felt urged to write a memoir about these 6 weeks of life-changing experiences before all the memories faded away. Partly to record the history, partly to preserve my perhaps immature and naïve scientific reflections on physics and biology.

There are many fables of scientists who, after fleeing from reality to somewhere far away, released a magical gush of creativity and produced amazing work that had been totally unexpected. The example dearest to me when I was still an atomic physicist was Heisenberg’s flight to Heligoland, an island in the North Sea, in the summer of 1925 to avoid pollen. He returned to announce the birth of quantum mechanics. In cell biology, the 6-week Physio course has also been spawning such fables for the past century. MBL really has the perfect setting for this—next to the ocean, in a super-small town, and isolated from the rest of the world. When Jim Watson was teaching Physio in 1954, Meselson was his TA and Stahl was a student; at Physio, they began the “most beautiful experiment in biology.” In 1983, cyclin, the molecule controlling the cell cycle, was discovered during the course, work that later led to a Nobel Prize. Cliff Brangwynne, now the director of the course, first observed the formation of liquid condensates in cells while attending it in 2008. Since then, condensates have become a major theme in cell biology.

While no one could really anticipate such things happening, the overall expectations were high. There were multiple alumni of the course in my group, including my PI. According to them, the 6 weeks would be very intense, challenging, and forever life-changing. We would rest only on Sundays, when the major task would be to compensate for all the sleep deprivation from Monday to Saturday. I was certainly very excited long before I arrived. Having transitioned only the previous summer from experimental atomic physics to the theoretical study of 3D genome organization, I was still new to biology. My aim was to gain broad exposure to wet-lab techniques and develop intuition for what happens at the bench, so that I could collaborate better with experimentalists. But really, deep down in my heart, I wanted to become more like a biologist by attending this course and less of a physicist. Before joining my current group, a prominent biophysicist asked me to think carefully about whether I wanted to study beautiful physics or biology. Indeed, it is hard to imagine that the ultra-diverse biomolecular complexes in organisms would all follow some esoteric mathematical symmetry or field-theoretical structures. I wanted to study material, objective nature itself instead of carving out some far-reaching corners of Hilbert space, as people had been doing with exotic quantum matter in my previous field. I wanted to speak the biological language better by totally immersing myself in the world of cell physiology. Admittedly, there was a little bit of zeal of the convert.

Skills Week

The first 2 weeks were the “skills weeks,” during which we learned different skills in modern cell biology through a combination of lectures and hands-on activities. They were split into several modules, each lasting 2–3 days. In the biochemistry module, we dissected live squid, extracted their optical globes, and purified microtubules and kinesin. In the microscopy module, we built our own TIRF microscope with Thorlabs components donated by Chen-Zuckerberg and based on a design from Tanner, the man behind light-sheet microscopy. Then we looked at our purified samples with the microscope that we had built! Unfortunately, very few subgroups did see microtubules gliding on kinesin, while many saw it on a commercial TIRF microscope. But still, good enough for a 2-day, home-built apparatus! Although we stayed up until almost midnight every day, there was quite a lot of waiting time between each step, so it was not that tiring. These time gaps were mostly filled with World Cup matches. Following biochemistry and microscopy came the theory module. I was initially very much not excited. David and Bob, who were fantastic biochemists and teachers, had just gotten me interested in learning more biochemistry, and I really wanted to learn more about buffers, proteins, etc. The content in this module was mostly from Physical Biology of the Cell, which I knew very well from preparing for my oral exam. There was also 10% of me that doubted whether this would ever succeed, because calculus takes longer to get used to than pipetting. I would soon hate myself for thinking this way.

The lecturers for this module, Christina and Deepak, were veterans of the course who were now starting their own labs. Christina started as a biologist, but she gradually learned physics. Deepak went the opposite way. They showed mastery of and great enthusiasm for pedagogy. I was very impressed by how much they covered in both breadth and depth: basic statistical mechanics, probability, random processes, continuum mechanics, and my favorite, information theory! To me, their success in achieving depth came from forcing us to calculate on our own, which is the most important part of learning any theory. Their success was also inseparable from their effort to place the theory in fundamental biological contexts (development/vision), relate it to biological phenomena basic enough to arouse immediate wonder (neuronal transport/elephant leg radius), or connect it to ongoing research that showed the power of the theoretical thinking they had just taught (condensates/the entire information theory module). Christina and Deepak really provided a model for how to convey our zeal and our wonder about the biological world in mathematical language that is neither dry nor terse, since math usually scares people off.

The influence of the theory module—and of Christina and Deepak—lasted throughout the course. Their account, in their final lecture, of how the course had influenced them was very touching. Their spirit was surely carried forward to the next generation of Physio: Mara and Karsh, my course mates who formed a similar pair—a physicist coming to biology and a biologist keen to learn physics—mimicked their iconic motion of tossing the laser pointer to each other in our rotation presentations. The estimation project, in which we all gave a 5-minute presentation in the style of Fermi problems, was a memorable night for many and kept resonating in our conversations until the end. (I presented with Jan and Daniel on whether we would get mercury poisoning before our skin turned red if we kept eating a flamingo diet!) According to the rotation PIs, this year they witnessed a major shift toward more modeling and theory in the rotation presentations compared with past years. Indeed, many course mates told me that their biggest scientific inspiration from the course was to apply more theory in their research. After all, the biophysical principles underlying cell physiology are not quantum field theory—they are intuitive, intimate, tangible, and fun.

In the omics module, we were very lucky to have an all-star crew of computational geneticists teaching us RNA-seq. The RNA samples were taken from the squid we had dissected the previous week. The lectures were amazing; the hands-on wet-lab portions were quite purposeful and well planned compared with the more exploratory biochemistry module, which I guessed was due to the nature of sequencing experiments. Nevertheless, under Sofi’s guidance, I felt quite satisfied to get a taste of many of the technical details in the whole process, especially the sampling problem associated with determining sequencing depth. RNA-seq actually felt more like experimental physics than other wet-lab work. The data-analysis portion (alignment, mapping, QC, etc.) was led by Troi. He was a very serious-looking man, but his jokes were extremely funny. At the end, Carry Albertin, a squid biologist, talked about the still-understudied genetics of marine animals. She got me very interested in the body plans of these non-model creatures. That night, I immediately downloaded Hi-C data around the HOX genes of many different organisms. How repressed HOX genes are gradually expressed to form the body plan was the initial motivation behind my thesis project on 3D epigenetic memory. After looking at these Hi-C maps for a bit, I wildly hypothesized that the HOX genes evolved from a more “1D-like” organization to a more “3D-like” organization as they became more clustered in the genome across the tree of life, because 3D epigenetics might support the development of more complex body plans. I kept discussing this possibility with Carry and Yiyan during our class trip to the MBL graveyard that night. Six Nobel laureates were buried there, but I could not really stop thinking and talking about my beloved HOX genes. Thanks to the course, the squid, and the Nobel laureates in the graveyard, I became a little more creative than usual.

The skills weeks ended with the image analysis, computer vision, and AI module, in which Beth patiently held our hands through cell segmentation on our laptops and the training of basic ML models, while Gaudenz gave us a series of phenomenal, thought-provoking talks. He was leaving academia to become a group leader at Roche Ltd. Therefore, his talks inevitably had a strong “farewell lecture” vibe: holistic reflections and coherent storytelling about everything he had engaged with throughout his career, ranging from ML algorithms to cell biology to causal inference. This is my favorite kind of talk, as it offers a rare chance to perceive someone’s scientific worldview as a whole. Pieces of knowledge can always be learned from ChatGPT, but it is the style of deep thinking that makes someone a unique scientist. I also just love hot takes, which Gaudenz loved to make. Out of respect for his strong distaste for social media and his request that his lecture material not be distributed, I shall stop here, except to end with a quote that I am sure all of us will remember even decades from now: “Do not ever, ever, ever, ever grow your cells on glass!” His eagle-like eyes might still secretly watch over our cells…

Rotation 1

I was assigned to Cliff Brangwynne’s group for this rotation. Cliff had a personality very similar to that of my undergraduate PI, so I immediately started to feel quite close to him. My overall goal was to understand the nucleolus and ribosomes better, in line with the spirit of the rotation projects: working on something entirely different from what we studied at our home institutions. In our group, the nucleolus has always been regarded as very mysterious. We never talk about it. To us, it is nothing more than excluded volume in the nucleus for the chromatin fiber. This is partly because we are a transcription-centric group, while the nucleolus is in charge of the other half of the Central Dogma, i.e., translation. It produces and assembles ribosomes, the executors of translation, by transcribing ribosomal RNA. But it is very mysterious indeed. The nucleolus, containing 2,000 protein species, can produce 100 ribosomes per second, but each ribosome takes 2 hours to exit the nucleolus—a slow but enormous fleet produced by an assembly line that is itself spontaneously assembled from 2,000 kinds of components into a structure with an unbelievably simple morphology. Self-evidently, these numbers already tell us how magical the nucleolus is—here again is the power of sheer numbers in biology that we had already appreciated in the estimation project with Christina and Deepak.

Unfortunately, I was initially not very motivated by the project I was assigned to. It was about inducing cell-cell fusion to create artificial syncytia. The original motivation was the following: back at Princeton, they were studying the dissolution of condensates by giving the cells a hypertonic shock to decrease the cytoplasmic protein concentration, but the cells were too stressed by the shock. Therefore, cell fusion might be a more physiologically permissive way to tune the protein concentration in the cytoplasm. I was entirely uninterested in this avenue. To me, this was still fundamentally an in-vivo-style in-vitro experiment, sweeping through different regions of phase space. This still felt like studying condensate physics rather than fundamental biology, and again, I did not want to work on physics. Another avenue was to use this as a tool to study the nucleolus. After the fusion of 2 or more cells, their original cytoplasms are mixed together, but each nucleus remains intact. The nucleolus exchanges material with the nucleoplasm, which in turn exchanges with the shared cytoplasm. This allows different nucleoli to “talk” to each other while also sharing a possibly larger burden of producing ribosomes in an enlarged cytoplasm. While the idea sounded cool, my concern was that our cell-fusion assay was quite “destructive” to the original cellular states, since it was inspired by viral infection. How physiologically relevant could my results be? I am not an immunologist; I want to study the nucleolus in “normal” cells. We also had little idea what kind of medium the shared cytoplasm provided for the nucleoli in the syncytium to communicate through, because so many things in the shared cytoplasm could change and so many pathways could be perturbed. Despite my dismay with the project on day 1, I was still lucky enough to have a fantastic TA. My TA, Hailey, was a graduate student in Cliff’s group. She was extremely nice, encouraging, and very experienced, saving me from many rounds of self-doubt over the coming 2 weeks.

We were asked to narrow the project down to a specific question by Friday, so that we could use the next week to explore it more deeply. My main goal was to narrow down what “communication” really meant. Given the cell lines that Hailey had prepared (there were really a lot! Ode to her detailed sample prep…), we conceived of 2 possible directions. The first was to study material exchange. We would fuse a cell line that overexpressed fluorescently tagged NPM1—the major scaffold protein of the nucleolus—with a wild-type (WT) cell line. We could look for fluorescent signals in the WT nucleoli after fusion; by themselves, these nucleoli should not contain any fluorescently tagged NPM1. The second was to treat cell fusion as a rescue experiment. We would fuse a cell line with an ill nucleolar state (an RNA Pol I degron, unable to transcribe rRNA and accompanied by an unhappy morphology) with a cell line containing untreated nucleoli, and see whether the untreated nucleolus could rescue the ill one through their communication, or whether the untreated one would be burdened to “yield more” because the ill one was nonfunctional.

The first week felt terrible. The feeling that I understood too little about an artificial system struck me hard. For the entire week, my main goal was to get a handle on the most basic phenomenology of cell fusion. How does cell fusion happen spatially? How long does it take for the cell membranes to break open and the cytoplasms to mix? What controls the size of the syncytium? Moreover, did the nucleoli show any signature of communication? If the answer was no, should I switch projects? (In retrospect, I now understand that even if the answer had been no, it would still have been a valuable answer. Nothing is trivial or obvious in cell biology until you actually show it! So there was really nothing to worry about.) And my “real” scientific question for the rotation was still to come. Due to the limited time, there was not much room for me to read or think at the beginning, only an immediate dive into experiments. On day 2, Hailey, Nima, another TA, and I walked out of the tissue-culture room at 6 a.m. This was the cumulative outcome of my slowness in pipetting, the large number of cell-fusion conditions placed in 96-well plates in order to find an optimal regime, and the fact that we were kicked out of the building at 4 a.m. because of a temporary gas leak that was resolved 1.5 hours later. After 2 hours of sleep, I felt 70% alive and kept the momentum going. Shamefully, despite all the hard work, I still could not answer any of the questions above by the end of the first week. I also started to doubt myself. I remember spending a whole day doing immunofluorescence (IF) for the first time. At the end of the day, I felt exhausted, only to realize that all I had done that day was complete one-third of a page of the protocol. On the last night of week 1, I was supposed to get my first real data from the fixed-cell plate and look at the outcome of the rescue experiment. It was an absolute failure: for 3 hours, I kept finding and then losing focus on the goddamn spinning-disk confocal microscope. I felt so humiliated that I woke up at 6 a.m. to re-image it. (It was not until the end of the rotation that I realized that answering the “basic” questions I had listed earlier was itself a whole separate project, which was eventually what my very diligent lab partner, Anna T., pursued. She started by studying the dissolution of condensates after fusion, which was the initial motivation of the project. But the condensates in her cell lines did not dissolve. She then used particle tracking to systematically characterize cytoplasmic exchange and captured good movies showing the spatial details of fusion. It really turned out that good live-cell fusion movies were not that easy to capture.)

Despite all the frustration, I did see something interesting. It came from my initial fusion experiment with the NPM1-overexpressing cell line. I did not see any fluorescence in the WT nucleoli hours after fusion, which suggested a total lack of movement of NPM1. This should have set off alarm bells for us, since it was a clear violation of the null hypothesis of concentration equilibration by diffusion. I instead dismissed it as a negative result for nucleolar communication—or simply as a sign that I had fucked something up. We quickly moved to the rescue experiments, where there might be a greater chance of seeing communication simply because the nucleolar differences between WT and the RNA Pol I degron cell line were more severe than the difference caused by NPM1 overexpression alone. I also performed 5-EU pulse labeling of RNA to use transcription as an orthogonal readout to morphology. It would be interesting, for example, to see the transcriptional activity of the RNA Pol I degron nucleolus—which by itself should not be transcribing—rescued while the morphology was not, or vice versa, since form and function are always considered coupled. The results were very hard to interpret. To my dismay, all the nucleoli looked quite similar in terms of their GC–DFC structure. I later realized that this was because both my untreated and degron lines overexpressed NPM1 for fluorescent tagging, while all previous characterizations of morphological damage due to the RNA Pol I degron had been done in true WT cells. Due to our inability to trace which cell line a nucleus had come from in our fused syncytia, and the limited effectiveness of the degron, the 5-EU data were also not really useful. Even population-level statistics would have been difficult, because they would have been confounded by the cell-death rate, which was quite high because of the stress from fusion.

It was finally time to think, and thinking helped a lot. Ned Wingreen, a prominent theorist from Princeton, came to join us as our theory TA for the second week. While we were talking about my nucleolar-communication business, Ned said the words “ground state,” and I suddenly realized what I thought was quite a neat scientific question: do intermediate nucleolar states exist? This question was motivated by the major conundrum of the nucleolus: its phase space should in principle be huge, simply because it is a 2,000-component condensate, whereas we saw only one type of nucleolar state in cells (the “ground state”). (I tried to make an analogy between this and the native state in protein folding, since I imagined both to be high-dimensional search processes, which was met with some appreciation. Nevertheless, by pitching this idea, I realized that what I had learned in my biophysics class about protein native states was very different from how biologists understood them. But what would be the chaperone for the nucleolus?) In nucleoli that lacked certain components because of degrons/knockouts, we could describe their morphologies using simple phrases such as “GC/DFC inverted” and “cauliflower-like,” suggesting that for these ill nucleoli, some kind of ground state also existed. Now, in our fused cells, some kind of “coupling”—a more general notion than mere communication—or coordination was imposed on 2 ground states by the shared cytoplasm. Would the 2 original ground states persist, or would they mix with each other and give birth to new, intermediate nucleolar states, as in the case of an avoided crossing in matrix (quantum) mechanics? To sound even fancier, do nucleoli have identities? Are they plastic? (I was satisfied to find that Ned, Cliff, and the TAs all showed interest in this question.) I performed IF + 5-EU on a new cell culture in which we fused WT cells with a TTF1 knockdown (KO) cell line that had a more distinct morphology than the previous degron line. To characterize the nucleolar state, I trained an autoencoder that learned a latent-space representation of the unfused WT and KO nucleoli. I could then compute the latent-space vector for each fused nucleolus and plot it together with the training data in UMAP space. Unfortunately, because of the limited time, I did not do a perfect job with cell segmentation and preprocessing, which made the autoencoder a little upset and made it hard to conclude whether there were new nucleolar states. At least it was clear that rescue did happen—more nucleoli were WT-like in the fused condition than in the control. Nevertheless, I think the idea was still solid and would deserve further exploration if my PhD were about the nucleolus.

At the same time, we also revisited the previous NPM1 finding, which Cliff thought was a big fish but which we had overlooked. As a reminder, we had seen a total absence of NPM1 exchange between the 2 nucleoli. I was advised to do a FRAP experiment on an individual nucleolus to show that the lack of exchange was not simply caused by NPM1 being sequestered within each nucleolus; instead, it could occur even though NPM1 exchanged rapidly between the nucleolus and nucleoplasm. Otherwise, the result would be boring: it would point to an artifact of the system rather than to potentially interesting biology. Indeed, after FRAP, each nucleolus recovered within a minute, supporting my previous observations. Now the story was complete, at least for the end-of-rotation 5-minute presentation: when cells containing multiple nucleoli were fused, the nucleoli could communicate, as evidenced by the rescue of function and morphology; in our search for the physical substrate of such communication, we found that their material exchange might be regulated, at least for NPM1. As expected, the shape of the story did not follow our actual scientific progress at all.

Rotation 2

Christina and Deepak always said, “On the shoulders of giants,” referring to the authors of PBoC. I was assigned to work with one of these giants—Hernan Garcia. In principle, I should not have been assigned to him, since he works on transcription in fly embryos using MS2, the same system that my lab mate Henrik, a Danish genius, also worked on, though in mice. However, my project for this rotation was about transcription factors (TFs). Although our group also studies transcription, we focus on 3D enhancer-promoter contact and how 3D genome organization influences transcription. TFs have always been intentionally ignored and renormalized into an effective rate (k_on) in our modeling. Thus, it was very different from my own research after all. In addition, all of our group’s collaborators grew cells on glass, whereas Hernan studies transcription in the context of embryo development. I was not at all a developmental biologist, but just as with the nucleolus, I expected to be transformed.

Classically, TF binding plays a key role in transcriptional activation. A central question about transcription in the fly embryo is: how does a smooth, diffusion-limited concentration gradient of several important TFs (e.g., Dorsal and Bicoid) across the embryo form a body plan with sharply demarcated boundaries? What is the input-output relation in transcription across the whole embryo, if the input is the Dorsal concentration at the gene and the output is the transcription level? While this is surely a major theme, another tentative project quickly caught my attention: studying the inhomogeneity of the TF distribution inside a single nucleus. It was interesting to me for 2 reasons. First, this problem was more fundamental to me (sorry, developmental biologists). When we speak of the TF-transcription input-output relation in embryo body-plan development, what actually is the input? The very statement of the dilemma of a smooth gradient versus a sharp developmental boundary seems to presuppose that the gene locus sees a single scalar value at one point on the gradient curve. But what if the input is a highly fluctuating time series, since the gene locus undergoes random movement in a spatiotemporally varying TF concentration field? Would the transcriptional outcome be effectively the same as if it saw the average value, or entirely different? Second, I thought this could be used to interrogate a fundamental question in chromatin biology that I have been quite fond of: is the nucleus more like a gel than a liquid? The idea that chromatin is gel-like has been gaining support through recent experiments such as liquid Hi-C. Chromatin takes up ~1% of the volume of a fly-embryo nucleus. What about the remaining 99%? We know that the nucleoplasm is very crowded and jammed, but does it all “jam together”? Does the TF concentration field move coherently? Thomas Gregor had recently posted a preprint about a coherent flow field acting on chromatin inside the nucleus and creating correlated noise; what about TFs? Moreover, does the TF field move together with the chromatin, or on its own, independently? This could tell us whether the inhomogeneity of the TF concentration in the nucleus is a result of chromatin binding or of TF self-organization, e.g., phase separation.

A short explanation of the system: the TF we looked at was Dorsal; however, I will simply call it “TF” because “dorsal” also describes a part of the fly embryo’s body. Strictly speaking, the TF binds to the enhancer, but in my system the enhancer is immediately proximal to the promoter, so this conceptual distinction does not make a functional difference for the question I am interested in; therefore, I will call it the “gene locus” throughout.

In the spirit of going for the big fish, I chose to start with the second idea. I was extremely lucky to have Aaron as my TA. He is a grad student in Hernan’s group and a person full of passion, curiosity, and warm encouragement. He really endorsed and supported me in exploring whatever interested me, unlike some other TAs who only made their students gather data for their machine-learning stuff. But this was the one time I wished I had received some discouragement from my TA, because my ambition also came from the false impression that embryo experiments were easier than cell-culture experiments, since embryos were simply gifts from Mother Nature that could be taken for granted. (That being said, had Aaron known about my overconfidence, he might still have told me to go for it.) The most natural first stab was to look at the autocorrelation function (ACF) at different positions in the nucleus. However, nothing useful could be inferred from my ACF plots. The reason could have been the following: during a nuclear cycle, the average nuclear concentration and the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio also change on a timescale of minutes. This creates a strong background autocorrelation—on a timescale where the phenomena we cared about might also occur, since plain diffusion takes seconds. Of course, there was surely much more to do, but we decided that, with the low photon counts, the signal would still be weak even if we knew what we were looking for.

The low photon counts have always been the biggest pain. It seems that nature likes to punish inquisitive scientists with ambiguous signals and a close-to-OK SNR that makes your hypothesis both right and wrong. This was also the case with my previous quantum-gas experiment. Technically, I believe our low photon counts were due to a combination of 2 factors. First, we were trying to image deep in z (it was an embryo, not a flat cell culture on glass!). When I imaged my embryo with my favorite scope from the last rotation, a Zeiss spinning disk, I could see only background autofluorescence and not a single nucleus. There were so many pinholes on the spinning disk that they degraded the rejection of out-of-focus light, and since spinning-disk scopes use cameras instead of photon counters, the limited photons are shared by all the camera pixels, resulting in low photon counts per pixel. A conventional laser-scanning microscope worked, but its signal quality was also not good enough because of scattering during deep imaging. Eventually, the Nikon NSPARC was the only microscope that could produce meaningful data for me. It was in the basement and had been flooded during Rotation 1, leading to several days of downtime. This time, I had to pray every day that it would not flood again and also fight with other groups when we signed up for microscope time for the next day. NSPARC has arrays of low-noise detectors that receive out-of-focus signals, which are subsequently used for background subtraction and super-resolution imaging. By the end of the course, NSPARC had gained some aura among us, partly because Margaret Gardel, the PI of another rotation group, saw my data and loved it so much that she started advertising it to her group members.

The second factor was that the fluorescent signal came only from adult flies eating Halo-dye-containing food and then transmitting the Halo dye to the embryo, where it bound to the Halo-tagged TF—so the TF molecules were only sparsely labeled. (There was an interesting interlude: we had previously had to limit the amount of dye we put in the fly food because the Halo dye was quite expensive. But one day, we magically found a box of dye in the freezer, so we started giving the flies twice as much dye in their food. However, the photon counts did not increase.) Plus, there were only about 2,000 of the TF molecules we were studying in each nucleus! For comparison, one nucleus had about 5,000 voxels. This 2,000-molecule problem is actually very interesting and fundamental to how TF-mediated transcriptional regulation works. There are many more DNA-binding sites than TF molecules in a nucleus. This fact has historically given rise to the fundamental question of how TFs bind specifically to chromatin. Kinetically, this means that the binding sites must compete with one another for this limited resource in order to achieve activation. How are these kinetics related to the spatial inhomogeneity of TFs? How are both related to the cooperative binding of TFs to DNA? And most importantly, although this is purely my hypothesis, could transcriptional bursting—or even bursty statistics in general, if we can ever claim such a subclass among non-Poissonian statistics—be a reactive strategy that genes have developed in response to their “starving” situation? Aaron and I conceived of single-body and many-body numerical simulations for these questions, and I am very excited to run them after the course.

Theoretical conjectures aside, it was time to move on to the more basic but equally interesting question: what does the gene “see,” i.e., what is the local environment that determines the TF input for the transcriptional output? To me, this question suggested a very natural hypothesis: does the gene have to experience an accumulation of TF before it starts to transcribe? Is there temporal gating of TF-driven transcriptional activation? Intuitively, answering this question should be quite straightforward. Just draw a small sphere around the gene-locus dot under the microscope in each frame, then count the total intensity of TF fluorescence in the sphere. There is one small problem and one bigger problem. The small problem is that we do not have a fluorescent marker for the gene’s position. Well, we still have the MS2 signal. It comes from nascent mRNA, which is spatially close enough to the gene. But this signal would generally disappear when the gene was not active, which was unfortunate for my question, since I needed to know precisely where the gene was before it turned on. Luckily, there were some tracks in which a turn-on event was preceded by a turn-off; after the gene had turned off, the RNA Pol II molecules were still gradually gliding off, so the remaining mRNA signal during this time window could still indicate the gene’s position before its next burst. The bigger problem is that, most of the time, these nuclei were in G2 phase, which means that there were 2 copies of the same gene being transcribed instead of one. Under the microscope, there would be 2 dots instead of one. Sometimes they were quite separated, and sometimes they overlapped. Even within the same frame, I could see this spatial fluctuation across the z-stack. It was therefore important to spatially resolve these 2 dots. With Codex, I worked out an algorithm that could reconstruct a 3D tube swept out by each dot’s trajectory during z-stack scanning. This would define a frame-rate-limited local TF environment that the gene locus sees. I found that, on average, there was a weak enrichment of TF concentration compared with the control. In some trajectories, I could clearly see a rise in TF concentration before burst onset, while in many others there was no trend at all. There are many other analyses that could be done on this. I took far more data than time permitted me to analyze fully. The plan is to continue it after I get back.

This rotation was a wonderful, well-rounded experience for me. At least, I became more familiar with the technical aspects of confocal microscopy, thanks to Zach, a microscope guru from Vanderbilt—also a school known for producing microscope gurus. Mounting embryos on coverslips was extremely difficult for me at the beginning, but who would have known that by the end of the rotation I would also become a mounting master! I even optimized the protocol a little and found a previously unnoticed bug. I got a taste of how to build good statistical controls for hypothesis testing in microscopy, which is very important for noisy data. Hernan’s group seemed to be quite professional at this. I tried to use a spherical-harmonic decomposition to show the inhomogeneity, because each z-slice looked like pure noise at such a low signal level. The results then needed to be compared with some control data. I shuffled the voxels on the same spherical shell. Julia, another TA, sharply pointed out that the closer a pixel was to the nucleus center (in XY coordinates), the more out-of-focus light the microscope would capture because it was deeper in z. Therefore, a more convincing control was to shuffle the pixels within the same annulus in each z-slice. Eventually, I did not present this approach because the spherical-harmonic method was a little too abstract for biologists. There was a fantastic trick—maximum z-projection—that immediately made the inhomogeneity manifest, even from 3 meters away. A good image is all you need. Scientifically, it was a truly stimulating 2 weeks. Besides merely massaging the embryo images to test our hypothesis at the computer, we all watched the whole process of embryo development while acquiring the data. Embryos are magical. The more we looked at them, the more we “discovered” amazing developmental processes. They might not have been immediately related to transcription, but once you detach from cell culture, every problem is fundamentally developmental. I would never have appreciated this if I had simply downloaded the processed time series from some Nature papers. Meanwhile, in the immediate vicinity of the exact question I was studying, Aaron and I kept throwing new ideas at each other. I was concerned that I might be doing this too much, but he reassured me: “That’s okay, because the good ones will just keep coming back to you.” Post-course research and communication have continued naturally, and we are both excited about that.

Final remarks

In the middle of Rotation 2, I was still struggling with mounting embryos and looked quite upset in the sample-prep room. Someone from my rotation group asked me, “Why are you so serious, Tingran?” But I was not the only one taking it very seriously. Although the purpose of Physio is to have fun, many people felt a great deal of pressure and self-doubt. (One day, the dog-food-serving dining hall played Queen’s “Under Pressure,” and we were like, “This is the song of the course.”) People were really hard on themselves. That pressure certainly crushed me during Rotation 1. My girlfriend came to see me, and we went back to Boston on Sunday. She pulled me into seeing a random ballet movie called Black Swan. The plot was about a ballet dancer placing impossibly high demands on herself while practicing for Swan Lake, which eventually led to her metamorphosis into a black swan. After watching this, I told my girlfriend that I should maybe also lower the standards I set for myself. After all, I am a theorist. But once I returned to MBL, the environment—the aura created by the people—immediately dispelled any thought of slacking off. The motivation would not fade. For me especially, another reason for driving myself like a machine was that the wet-lab experiments I did at Physio might be the last wet-lab experiments I would do for the rest of my life. I may do experiments during my postdoc, but who knows whether I will even do a postdoc or not? Seize the day! I knew that some people, including me, began to feel quite unwell (e.g., heart discomfort) toward the end because of the continuous sleep deprivation.

The purpose of elaborating on the stress is not to elicit sympathy. In fact, we were truly privileged to have the chance to be so immersed in pure science for 6 weeks. The end of the course meant returning to reality for many of us—failing projects, publishing papers, looking for postdocs, and all kinds of responsibilities. For those 6 weeks, however, we had the golden chance to begin independent work directly on projects whose scientific mysteries were still largely unresolved, while the “dirty work”—e.g., making cell lines, protocol optimization, protein purification, and quality control—had already been done long before the course began. In Lucas Pelkman’s rotation group, the TA spent 3 months purifying DRK3, and they brought all the aliquots they had made for the students in their group to microinject into oocytes. Most TAs also stayed up super late with us every day to support us or, in my case, to babysit me. Some of us also had a chance to use fantastic instruments, thanks to the culture MBL had created, which encouraged companies to test their state-of-the-art instruments there. Many of us got to use arguably the best super-resolution microscope for a week (from CSR). One team in Hernan’s rotation group used a $500,000 embryo-sorting machine for its high-throughput screening experiments. Given the increasingly difficult funding situation, this was even more precious. (The national research-funding cuts had also shortened this program by 1 week. Next year, it would be shortened by yet another week. It is worrying whether Physio can remain financially sustainable in the near future.)

These 6 weeks felt long. Much longer than 6 weeks. MBL felt like the Der Zauberberg (Magic Mountain) of science. In Magic Moutain, the protagonist went into an Alpine sanatorium to look after his cousin who had TB. He planned to stay there for 3 weeks, but he stayed there for 7 years. During these 7 years, he was totally immersed in art and philosophy. Time was very non-linear in this timeless bubble. He finally stepped out of the sanatorium to be welcomed by World War I, which we all knew what happened. This was exactly how I felt after the course ended. When I went back to Boston, I could not even tell how much time has flown past. Part of me has been forever left at MBL that would never come back. I also quickly learned about the news that GPT 5.6 has just cracked modern mathematical sciences. We might have just witnessed the last human-awarded Fields Medal. Indeed, many people, including leading figures in the course, were under the same sensation that LLMs would make it harder for theorists or pure computational scientists to survive in the future. However, among physicists who were serious about agentic AI that I knew off, it was the total opposite. Modern physics experiments are quickly becoming highly “agentized” way faster than theory, thanks to their machine-controlled nature. The automation of biological sample preparation would involve more robotic developments than LLMs. But sample prep, at least in cell biology, is basically just transferring liquid and not really fun. The fun really starts when the sample was transferred to the microscope. That is where the biology begins for the biologists. It is this fun part that is most prone to AI takeover, not the mundane laboring. As far as I know, no one has seriously worked on this, even OpenAI, but no one could fight against progress. One rotation PI, Manu Prakash, brought his custom microscope and MCP to the course. One night, he demonstrated interacting with Claude Code using a sample directly taken from the pond. One example prompt was, “find the Stentor, focus on it and zoom in.” Claude struggled, took some time, but eventually did it. It was slow only because Claude models over-optimized for coding instead of multimodal reasoning. In all, there are still a lot of room for development, but I am sure agentic microscopes would be achieved soon. I was a little surprised to learn about this project, because Manu looked like a Miyazaki-type scientist – I used this analogy because I loves Miyazaki. He respects nature, goes on fierce voyages to capture the weirdest creatures in the oceans and presents them without losing their beauty. (One of his rotation projects was about capturing fireflies. They posted fliers on the street for 2$ per firefly captured. At the end-or-rotation presentation, they invited all the kids who participated. It was very touching.) Max Weber argued in Science as a Vocation that scientists use science to disenchantment, or “de-magic-ation” of the world. To me, Manu proved he was wrong by demonstrating the power of observation. But that simple prompt above he gave to Claude contains all that confers such power of microscopy. After AI occupies this part, would doing cell biology stop being fun? To be clear, these are by no means any form of criticism to Manu. We need to keep developing AI even though it is a threat because it would boost our productivity by so much. Hypocritically, I am myself an agentic developer, and during the course I have been collecting agentic benchmarks for bio-imaging/microscopy harnesses to train next-level foundational models for cell biology. I have also advocated it so much that everyone in our cohort knew me as a big fan of Codex-driven data analysis. Would science still be the kind of science we are familiar with? Would biology still be the same biology that attracted me to switch from quantum physics?
